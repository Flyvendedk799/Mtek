# 0099. What the first runtime benchmark showed about Mtek, what was and was not changed, and what the mount/reload/dispose plateaus consist of

- Status: Accepted
- Date: 2026-10-19
- Blueprint origin: §12.3, M7; `spec/ai-and-benchmarks.md` section 7; decisions 0059 (instancing), 0095 to 0098.
- Supersedes: nothing.

## Context

Task M7-04 produced the first runtime measurements of Mtek. The report (`benchmarks/results/runtime/report.md`, generated from `raw/20261019T052132Z/`, one run on an AMD Radeon RX 7900 XTX under Chromium 153, definitions frozen as `eb4cc942...`) holds every number; this record states what they mean for the runtime, which observations are defects or design costs, and what the plateau tables consist of. Numbers quoted here are from that run and are not a speed guarantee (section 7 of the spec; decision 0096 item 7). Statements are labelled: **observation** (measured in this run), **hypothesis** (not tested), **decision** (what was done).

## Decision

1. **Observation: Mtek exceeds the 20 % investigation threshold against the direct-WebGPU reference in six of seven workloads** (the report's summary table): the constant scene (p95 frame time 0.115 ms against 0.035 ms), 1,000 changing transforms (1.360 ms against 0.145 ms), 500 changing material params (0.670 ms against 0.150 ms), 2,000 repeated meshes (0.245 ms against 0.065 ms), 300 physics bodies (1.830 ms against 1.320 ms) and the mount/reload/dispose cycles (23.45 ms against 10.65 ms); the asset-heavy startup is within it (p95 223.9 ms against 204.4 ms, +9.6 %). The absolute differences are small (0.08 to 1.2 ms per frame on a 16.7 ms budget); the ratios are not. The three.js baseline is slower than the direct reference in all five frame workloads too. Against three.js (a comparison the spec's threshold does not ask for, shown in the report) Mtek is faster in the constant scene (0.115 ms against 0.430 ms), the transforms, the material workloads and the physics scene (1.830 ms against 2.105 ms), and slower in the repeated-meshes workload (0.245 ms against 0.130 ms, where three.js uses its `InstancedMesh` with matrices written once).
2. **Observation: the cost is on the CPU, not the GPU.** GPU time from `timestamp-query` is a few microseconds for the scenes that draw with one or a few instanced draws (0.004 to 0.009 ms in Mtek and direct), 0.019 ms for the 500-draw material scene in both Mtek and three.js against 0.005 ms for direct's single draw, and 0.027 to 0.029 ms for the textured spheres in all three. Draw calls per frame are in the report: Mtek groups compatible entities (decision 0059) into 8 instanced draws in the constant scene and 1 in the transforms and repeated-meshes scenes, 4 in the physics scene, and draws 500 individually where every box has its own parameter block, as three.js does.
3. **Observation: what Mtek does per frame that the reference does not** (from the counters of the same run). (a) **One `writeBuffer` per changed entity**: 1,001 calls and 250.0 KiB per frame for the 1,000 moving boxes (an object block of 128 bytes each, plus the instance buffer range), 301 calls and 75.0 KiB for the 300 bodies, against one call and 62.5 KiB or 18.8 KiB for the direct reference. The arena uploads dirty slots one by one (decision 0059 item 6, `uniform-arena.ts`). (b) **A per-frame pass over every entity even when nothing changed**: the constant scene spends 0.065 ms of render preparation (the reference: 0.020 ms) on culling, building and batching the draw list and comparing the instance records. (c) **JavaScript allocation**: 35.2 KiB per frame in the constant scene (23.0 KiB of it `Float32Array.prototype.subarray` views, 5.7 KiB in `cull`), 157.2 KiB for the 2,000 instances, 1.77 MiB per frame for the 1,000 moving boxes (`propagate`, `setTransform`, `normalMatrix`, `writeObjectBlocks`, `qaxisAngle`), against 0.6 to 184 KiB for the direct reference (the sampling profile's largest sites are printed per workload in the report). three.js allocates 14 to 320 KiB in the same workloads. Allocation of this size per frame is a source of garbage-collection pauses, which show in the p99 columns. (d) **Startup**: Mtek's mount takes 118.5 ms in the constant scene against 64 ms for the other two, its runtime is 754.7 KiB unminified (the shipped v0.1 form; 583.6 KiB minified for the whole constant-scene program, 91.5 KiB gzip) against 13.4 KiB for the hand-written reference and 2.07 MiB (772 KiB minified) for three.js; the 4.2 MiB physics module with the Rapier engine is loaded lazily.
4. **Observation: no correctness defect was found.** Every implementation of every workload ran in every pass without a page error, an uncaptured WebGPU validation error or a device loss; the three implementations drew pictures that agree to within the tolerance in every workload (at most one pixel, 0.0004 % of the picture, in one workload, the colour one, differs by more than it: by a channel value of 220; its position was not examined); Mtek's own `drawCalls` and `pipelinesCreated` agree with the instrument in all seven workloads; and the registry's live counters are zero after `dispose()` in every one of the 50 cycles and in all 800 of the supplementary run.
5. **Decision: nothing in the runtime or the compiler was changed in this task.** The findings in item 3 are performance characteristics of the design (per-slot uploads, a per-frame pass over all entities, allocation in the transform and culling paths, an unminified runtime), not bugs with a one-line fix and a test; changing them after the definitions were frozen and the numbers taken would also make the report describe code that no longer exists. They are candidates for follow-up work, in the order the counters suggest: coalesce contiguous dirty slots into one `writeBuffer`; avoid the per-entity typed-array views and vector objects in `cull`, `propagate` and `setTransform`; skip the instance-record comparison and the cull for entities whose world matrices did not change; ship a minified runtime. Each would be measured again with this benchmark; none is promised here.
6. **What the plateau tables consist of** (the question of task M7-04: do the numbers plateau?). **Observation:** *everything the application can release is released, in every cycle*: the GPU buffer and texture bytes not destroyed after `dispose()`, the devices requested and not destroyed, and for Mtek every live counter of the registry (buffers, textures, samplers, shader modules, pipelines, bind groups, listeners) are exactly zero after every dispose, in the 50-cycle workload and in the 800-cycle run; DOM nodes and event listeners settle after the first few cycles (Mtek: 11 to 12 nodes and 14 to 15 listeners from cycle 10 on, the same as the baseline pages plus the page's own handles) and stay. The creations per cycle are the same every cycle (the report's table). **The JavaScript heap in use after a forced collection does not plateau by the rule of decision 0098 in the 800-cycle run, in any of the three implementations** (in the 50-cycle workload the direct reference's heap is a plateau, +0.7 %, and Mtek's and three.js's are classed growing, +3.0 % and +1.4 %): it rises in the first cycles (Mtek from 3.27 MiB at cycle 1 to 4.59 MiB at cycle 10: module code, caches and lazily created tables), then in the 800-cycle run keeps rising **in steps** (the median change per cycle is 0 B in all three, the largest single-cycle increase is 439.6 KiB for Mtek, 72.7 KiB for direct and 188.0 KiB for three.js); over the whole run the heap grew by 2.89 MiB for Mtek, 334.8 KiB for direct WebGPU and 1.39 MiB for three.js.
7. **Observation: what that growth is.** A heap-snapshot comparison between cycle 100 and cycle 400 (`tools/heap-diff.ts`, output in `benchmarks/results/runtime/heap-diff/`, one run per implementation, not part of the frozen protocol) shows that **no JavaScript object type grew**: the nodes that grew by more than 2 KB are compiled code (`InstructionStream`, `TrustedByteArray`, `ProtectedFixedArray`: 295 KB for Mtek, 106 KB for direct, 177 KB for three.js over 300 cycles) and the browser's own bookkeeping that exists because a DevTools session is attached (`blink::ConsoleMessage` and `SourceLocation`, one per cycle for Mtek and direct; `blink::NetworkResourcesData::ResourceData`, 346 KB for Mtek, four per cycle: the manifest and shader files Mtek fetches at every mount and reload). **Hypothesis (not tested):** the steps are V8 allocating heap pages for code and the inspector's retained records, so the growth is an artefact of measuring through the DevTools protocol and of JIT code, not a retention in Mtek's objects; a run without a DevTools session would have to be read through another instrument to confirm it, and 300 cycles of snapshots cannot exclude a leak smaller than the 2 KB threshold per object type. What the data supports is: Mtek does not accumulate GPU resources, DOM nodes or listeners across mount/reload/dispose cycles, and the JavaScript heap growth it shows is the same kind of growth the hand-written direct reference shows, larger because Mtek fetches more per cycle and compiles more code.
8. **Decision: the measurement mistakes found while preparing this benchmark are recorded, not hidden.** (a) The page first kept one record per cycle in its own memory, which grew every implementation's heap by about 2 KB per cycle and made the direct reference look as if it leaked; it was removed before the frozen run (decision 0098 item 7). (b) npm placed the pinned Rapier 0.21.0 below `benchmarks/node_modules` while the repository root holds 0.12.0 for another package; the direct and three.js implementations resolve 0.21.0 (the same as Mtek's physics adapter), which a unit test pins (`tools/versions.test.ts`), and a first version of the preparation step recorded the root's 0.12.0 as the version used, which was a mislabel of the report only and was corrected before the frozen run. (c) A GPU lock taken per pass starved the run on a shared machine (decision 0095 item 12). (d) One direct-WebGPU timing run of the physics workload ran at 93 % machine load and moved a median; the rule of repeating disturbed passes (decision 0095) was added, and 14 of the 413 passes of the run (nine startup loads, four timing runs and one GPU-time pass) were repeated, all calm on the second attempt, with every attempt kept in the raw results.

## Consequences

- The report's threshold column says six of seven workloads exceed 20 %: that is the headline, not a footnote, and release copy must not claim runtime performance relative to hand-written WebGPU. The absolute per-frame costs are small and the scenes were chosen to stress the CPU path, not to represent applications.
- The follow-up candidates of item 5 are the next performance work; a future run under a recorded change of the definitions (decision 0097) or of the runtime (the tool records the commit) shows whether they moved the numbers.
